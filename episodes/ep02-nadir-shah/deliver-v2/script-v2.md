## B1 · 1719 · The Pleasure King  (00:18)

- `00:18.3` To answer that, meet four men: an emperor who loved pleasure, a conqueror in a hurry, a governor caught between them, and a minister who had already half walked away.
- `00:28.8` The emperor first. Muhammad Shah took the Mughal throne in 1719, and he held it for twenty-nine years.
- `00:36.6` People called him Rangeela, the colourful one, for his love of music, poetry and a luxurious court.
- `00:43.6` Only a few years before him, powerful nobles had been making and breaking emperors at will. Muhammad Shah got free of them early in his reign, but the throne never fully recovered its authority.
- `00:56.2` But while the music played, the empire's great governors were quietly turning into kings.
- `01:01.9` In 1724, Nizam-ul-Mulk, once the emperor's own chief minister, set up his own state in Hyderabad.
- `01:10.6` Bengal, Awadh and Punjab drifted the same way. They still bowed to Delhi, but they kept their taxes and their armies close.
- `01:19.4` So by the 1730s the Mughal Empire still looked enormous on a map. In reality, it was a throne resting on borrowed shoulders.

## B2 · 1737 · Warning Shots  (01:29)

- `01:29.4` Far from the court, the north-west frontier, India's oldest gateway for invaders, was left neglected.
- `01:36.6` But the first warning came from the south.
- `01:39.5` In 1737, the Maratha Peshwa, Baji Rao the First, rode north and struck at the very edge of Delhi.
- `01:48.0` Rajiv Ahir's history says he reached the capital with just five hundred horsemen. Other accounts describe a much larger Maratha army behind him.
- `01:58.9` Either way, the message was the same: the emperor could not protect his own capital.
- `02:04.5` Baji Rao rode home, but the lesson stayed behind.
- `02:08.7` ★ Delhi had just been shown how weak it was. Someone in Persia was watching.

## B3 · 1738 · The Storm  (02:14)

- `02:14.7` That someone was Nadir Shah.
- `02:17.3` He was a soldier who had fought his way to the crown of Persia in 1736. He was fast, he was ruthless, and his wars needed money.
- `02:27.0` India, rich but divided, looked like the answer.
- `02:30.6` In 1738 his army crossed the old border into Mughal lands and took Kabul. Early in 1739, Lahore fell.
- `02:40.3` In Delhi, the court argued about who should lead, and the old Nizam's advice was heard but not trusted.
- `02:47.4` Now Muhammad Shah had to do the one thing he had avoided all his life. He had to go to war.

## B4 · 1739 · Karnal  (02:54)

- `02:54.3` In February 1739, the two sides met at Karnal, about a hundred and twenty kilometres north of Delhi.
- `03:02.5` The Mughal camp was enormous, by one count three hundred thousand people, including servants and camp followers.
- `03:10.1` But Nadir's army was smaller, faster and better drilled, with uniformed musketeers and light guns carried on camels.
- `03:18.7` Saadat Khan, the governor of Awadh, rode out against the Persians and was captured.
- `03:24.4` Within hours, the Mughal army was broken.
- `03:27.7` Muhammad Shah was taken into Nadir's camp. The emperor of India had become a guest who could not leave.
- `03:35.1` ★ The battle at Karnal was only the beginning. The real damage happened inside Delhi.

## B5 · 1739 · Delhi  (03:42)

- `03:42.2` On the twentieth of March, Nadir Shah rode into Delhi as its master.
- `03:47.2` The next night, a rumour spread that he had been killed, and some of his soldiers were attacked in the streets.
- `03:53.8` On the morning of the twenty-second of March, he ordered his troops to strike back at the city.
- `03:59.6` In a few hours, an estimated twenty to thirty thousand people of Delhi were killed. The true number will never be known.
- `04:07.8` Doors stayed shut. The markets fell silent.
- `04:11.1` Even so, the survivors were ordered to pay an enormous fine.

## B6 · 1739 · The Loot  (04:16)

- `04:16.0` But the real prize was the treasury.
- `04:18.9` Nadir's men emptied the imperial vaults and the private safes of the nobles.
- `04:24.5` By one estimate in Ahir's history, the haul was worth seventy crore rupees. A crore is ten million.
- `04:31.7` He took the Peacock Throne of Shah Jahan, the Kohinoor, and another great diamond, the Darya-i-Noor, the Sea of Light.
- `04:39.8` The demands fell hardest on the nobles who had promised him money. Saadat Khan was one of them. The book says that, under that pressure, he took his own life.
- `04:50.3` Persia also took every Mughal land west of the river Indus, Kabul included.
- `04:56.1` Before he left, Nadir placed the crown back on Muhammad Shah's head. He left him on a throne without its treasure.
- `05:03.5` ★ Nadir Shah went home in May. But he had shown everyone — Afghans, Marathas, even a company of merchants — that Delhi could be taken.

## B7 · Aftershocks  (05:13)

- `05:13.9` The historian Jon Wilson, in his book India Conquered, puts it like this.
- `05:19.1` “Nader Shah was Mughal emperor for only fifty-seven days, in 1739, but those days created aftershocks that transformed India's politics…
- `05:30.7` Those fifty-seven days laid the ground which allowed the East India Company to conquer territory in India for the first time.”
- `05:39.2` How could a Persian raid help a British trading company?
- `05:43.1` Because the money was gone. Delhi could no longer pay its armies or reward the governors who stayed loyal.
- `05:50.1` So the governors stopped waiting for orders. Hyderabad, Bengal and Awadh ran their own affairs, and the Marathas pushed further north.
- `05:59.3` In a land of rival rulers, a company with ships, guns and hired soldiers could pick a side, and in time take land for itself.
- `06:08.4` Not every historian gives fifty-seven days that much weight. Many point to a decline that began long before Nadir Shah.
